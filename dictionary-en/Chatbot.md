A chatbot is a program you talk to in writing: you type a question, it answers in text. The term is old and covers very different things, from the help window on a company website to ChatGPT.

Classic chatbots work from fixed rules. They spot keywords such as “opening hours” or “reset password” and return a prepared text. Type something that is not on their list and you get “Sorry, I did not understand that”. Bots like these have been sitting in customer service, IT help desks and HR portals for years. They do not understand anything, they match.

The new chatbots are built on a [language model](./Sprachmodell.md). ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and the chat in Microsoft 365 Copilot belong to this group. They have no list of answers; they generate the text fresh for every request. That is why you can phrase things freely, ask follow-up questions, switch topics and have an email rewritten without anyone having planned for that case in advance. This makes them useful, but it also brings the familiar weaknesses: they can produce errors that sound convincing, see [hallucination](./Halluzination.md).

You can tell the two apart quickly. A rule-based bot always gives the same answer to the same question and often offers buttons to choose from. An AI chatbot words its answer a little differently each time and picks up on the [chat](./Chat.md) so far, meaning what you wrote earlier.

At work, the AI chatbot is usually the starting point: you open a window, ask a question, get a draft. What it cannot do on its own is reach your files, emails or systems. That takes more, see [AI assistant](./KI-Assistent.md).

_Common misconception:_ “The chatbot on our website is AI too.” Usually not. Many website bots are still rule-based and share nothing with ChatGPT except the chat window.

_In practice:_

> “The bot in the customer portal doesn’t get it again, I’ve rephrased three times.”
>
> “That’s still an old rule-based chatbot, it only knows its keywords. Try the Copilot chat, that’s an AI chatbot and it copes with free text.”
